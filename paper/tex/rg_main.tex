\begin{abstract}
Ice nucleation is read from primitive rings, which form the hexagonal cages of ice~Ih and the double-diamond cages of ice~Ic.
d-SEAMS~2.0 is the first code to return a full ring search from a local update, and on one thread a \dsv{hl@n} molecule ice~Ic frame takes \qty{\dsv{hl@two@ms}}{\milli\second} against \qty{\dsv{hl@one@s}}{\second} for d-SEAMS~1.
Rings are pairs of shortest paths, indexed from each molecule.
A ring of at most seven members changes only at an end of a changed bond, so the carried rings match a full search.
Three changed bonds make the update \dsv{hl@ringgain} times faster than a full search.
In all \dsv{hl@matched} matched comparisons of the main campaign, d-SEAMS~2.0 is faster than the Open Visualization Tool (OVITO), freud, pyscal, matscipy and a cubic hexagonal interfacial liquid (CHILL) library.
On a deposited mW run, most new ice is cubic or shared by both cage types.
\end{abstract}

{\em Solution method:}
Primitive rings are built from pairs of shortest paths in the bond graph [2,3], so each ring is found once, from its lowest-numbered member, and tested for shortcuts against distance rows of bounded radius.
An index from each molecule to the six-membered rings through it turns the searches for hexagonal and double-diamond cages [1,4] into passes that are linear in the number of rings.
The double-diamond cages are packed as a maximum matching with a certificate of optimality.
Between frames, the program carries every ring of up to seven members whose molecules keep all their bonds and searches again only from the molecules whose bonds changed, which gives the rings of a full search.
The cage labels are updated in the same way, from the rings near a changed bond or a changed ring.
The ring update takes a full pass only when every molecule is within reach of a changed row.
The cage-label update returns to the full search once the changed share of the molecules passes a fixed threshold.
The program also finds the prism blocks of ice in nanotubes from pairs of stacked rings and the clathrate cages from their face signatures.\\

The program reads LAMMPS dump, XYZ and CON files, and it runs as a command-line program, as a C++ library and through its C interface.
A LAMMPS compute assigns CHILL+ labels during a run.
Rings of more than seven members are carried only when they also avoid a margin around the molecules whose bonds changed.

A component whose conflicts are not those of a bipartite graph is solved by branch and bound on a bit set with one bit per candidate, so the set grows with the component and no component is dropped.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Molecular dynamics follows the freezing of water through the positions of its molecules, so the structure of a growing ice nucleus has to be read from each saved frame of a trajectory~\cite{matsumoto2002molecular,moore2011structural}.
Nucleation rates and critical nucleus sizes come from long runs or from forward-flux and seeding calculations that analyse many frames~\cite{li2011homogeneous,haji2015nucleation,espinosa2016seeding}.
The polytype of the nucleus is part of the answer, since stacking-disordered ice, built from cubic and hexagonal layers, gives a nucleus that is more stable than either pure polytype in the mW model~\cite{lupi2017stacking}.
Every such study assigns a structure to every molecule of every frame, and the cost of that assignment multiplies with the length and the number of the runs.

Most analysis codes classify molecules by local order parameters.
These include the bond-orientational averages of Steinhardt and of Lechner and Dellago~\cite{steinhardt1983bond,lechner2008averaged,lechner2008accurate}, the CHILL and CHILL+ bond correlations~\cite{moore2010chill,nguyen2015identification} and the template and neighbour analyses of crystal structures~\cite{larsen2016robust,stukowski2012structure,maras2016global}.
The Open Visualization Tool OVITO, freud and pyscal implement them for any particle system~\cite{stukowski2010visualization,ramasubramani2020freud,menon2019pyscal}.
Their labels depend on the neighbour list that defines the local environment, and ice in nanotubes has no structure class among them~\cite{koga2001nanotube,goswami2020dseams}.

Topological criteria read the structure from the rings of the hydrogen-bond network instead~\cite{king1967ring,guttman1990ring,franzblau1993computation}.
Ice~Ih is built from hexagonal cages and ice~Ic from double-diamond cages, both made of six-membered rings~\cite{haji2015nucleation}, ice in a nanotube from stacked prism blocks~\cite{koga2001nanotube,goswami2020dseams} and clathrate hydrates from polyhedral cages of five- and six-membered rings~\cite{jacobson2010amorphous,matsumoto2007topological}.
Ring statistics codes for network solids count these rings, among them RINGS, ISAACS, atomes and matscipy~\cite{leroux2010rings,leroux2010isaacs,leroux2025atomes,grigorev2024matscipy}, the cycless package that GenIce uses~\cite{matsumoto2026cycless,matsumoto2018genice} and the ring finder of OVITO~\cite{ovitofindrings}.
Each of them searches every frame from the start and stops at the ring counts.

d-SEAMS~1~\cite{goswami2020dseams} brought the topological criteria for bulk and confined ice together in one program.
It finds rings by backtracking over every path up to the largest ring size and then removes the rings with shortcuts.
It tests every pair of six-membered rings for a hexagonal cage and every ring against every other for a double-diamond cage.
Its cage searches therefore grow with the cube and the square of the number of molecules, and one frame of \dsv{hl@n} molecules of ice~Ic takes \qty{\dsv{hl@one@s}}{\second} on one core (Section~\ref{sec:versions}).
Two frames saved a few steps apart share most of their bonds, yet each frame is searched from the start.

Dynamic graph algorithms update shortest paths after a change of edges instead of recomputing them~\cite{demetrescu2004new}.
For primitive rings the question is local, because a ring gains or loses its status only through a shortcut, and a short ring admits only short shortcuts.
We make this locality exact: a ring of at most seven members changes its status only if one of its molecules is an end of a changed bond, so every other ring can be carried to the next frame.

Here, we describe d-SEAMS~2.0, called d-SEAMS below, which keeps the topological definitions of d-SEAMS~1 and replaces its algorithms.
d-SEAMS is the first code in this comparison to return the primitive rings of a full search from a local update, and the rings and both cage searches of a \dsv{hl@n}-molecule ice~Ic frame take \qty{\dsv{hl@two@ms}}{\milli\second} against \qty{\dsv{hl@one@s}}{\second} for d-SEAMS~1.
It constructs primitive rings from pairs of shortest paths~\cite{yuan2002rings}, so that each ring is found once.
It finds both cage types through an index from molecules to rings, packs the double-diamond cages as a maximum matching and carries rings from the molecules within reach of a changed row.
The cage labels are carried in the same way, and that update returns to the full search at a fixed share of changed rows.
We compare it with its predecessor and with seven other codes on machines of one type.
On a nucleation trajectory of mW water, it finds the growing ice to be built mostly from cubic and mixed molecules.
Unlike the ring statistics codes, d-SEAMS assembles the rings into cages, and unlike the local classifiers, it takes its labels from the topology of the network.

\section{Definitions and algorithms}
\label{sec:defs}

\subsection{Bond graphs}
\label{sec:graphs}

A frame gives the positions of $n$ water molecules in a periodic cell, each represented by its oxygen atom.
The bond graph $G=(V,E)$ has one vertex per molecule and an edge, called a bond, between two molecules whose oxygen atoms lie within the cutoff $r_c=\qty{\dsv{set@cutoff}}{\angstrom}$ under the minimum image convention, as in earlier topological analyses~\cite{goswami2020dseams,moore2010chill}.
A cutoff graph admits interstitial molecules and misses stretched bonds, so we also build two graphs from the four nearest neighbours of each molecule.
The mutual graph joins two molecules when each is among the four nearest neighbours of the other, and the union graph joins them when either is.
Seeded labels take the cage labels of the mutual graph as seeds and accept a cage label of the union graph only when its cluster of labelled molecules contains a seed.
The extra bonds of the union graph then count only where they extend a structure of the mutual graph.
We call this pair of graphs the seeded graph, the default of the cage labels, and every result below names its graph.

\subsection{Primitive rings}
\label{sec:rings}

A ring $R=(v_0,\dots,v_{k-1})$ is a cycle of $k$ distinct vertices of $G$, and $d_R(u,v)$ is the number of bonds between its members $u$ and $v$ along the shorter arc of the ring.
Following the shortest-path criterion of King and Franzblau~\cite{king1967ring,franzblau1993computation,goswami2020dseams}, we call $R$ primitive when no path through the network joins two of its members in fewer bonds than the ring does,
\begin{equation}
  d_G(u,v)=d_R(u,v)\quad\text{for all } u,v\in R,
  \label{eq:primitive}
\end{equation}
where $d_G$ is the distance in $G$.
A path that is shorter than the ring arc is a shortcut, and a ring with a shortcut is the sum of two smaller rings, so it is not counted.
On the wall of a hexagonal ice nanotube, the six-cycle around two neighbouring squares has a shortcut along the bond the squares share, so Eq.~\eqref{eq:primitive} keeps the squares and rejects the six-cycle (Section~\ref{sec:example}).

Algorithm~\ref{alg:rings} enumerates the primitive rings of up to $D$ members with the construction of Yuan and Cormack~\cite{yuan2002rings}, and we write the size of a ring as $k=2\ell$ or $k=2\ell+1$.
For a primitive ring with lowest member $s$ and even size $k=2\ell$, both arcs from $s$ to the opposite member $p$ are shortest paths of $G$.
For odd $k=2\ell+1$, the arcs from $s$ to the two ends $p$ and $q$ of the opposite bond are shortest paths.
The algorithm therefore grows a breadth-first tree from each vertex $s$ over the vertices above $s$, closes pairs of disjoint shortest paths into candidate rings and keeps a candidate that satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:primitive}.
Because the tree contains only $s$ and vertices above $s$, the vertex $s$ is the lowest member of every ring it closes, and each ring is found once.
The test of Eq.~\eqref{eq:primitive} needs distances only up to $\max(\ell-1,1)$ from each member, since a shortcut is shorter than an arc of at most $\ell$ bonds.
In a network of bounded degree, the work per vertex depends only on the degree and on $D$, so the search is linear in $n$.
Section~\sref{sec:s-rings} shows that it finds every primitive ring of up to $D$ members exactly once.
The search of d-SEAMS~1 instead follows every path of up to $D-1$ bonds from each vertex by backtracking and removes the rings with shortcuts afterwards~\cite{goswami2020dseams}, while the trees of Algorithm~\ref{alg:rings} stop at depth $\lfloor D/2\rfloor$.

\begin{algorithm}[tbp]
\caption{Primitive rings of a bond graph $G$ up to size $D$.}
\label{alg:rings}
% snapper:off
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{each vertex $s$ of $G$}
\State Grow a breadth-first tree from $s$ over the vertices above $s$ to depth $\lfloor D/2\rfloor$, keeping every shortest-path parent.
\For{each vertex $p$ at depth $\ell$ and each pair of disjoint shortest paths from $s$ to $p$}
\State Close the two paths into a candidate ring of $2\ell$ members.
\EndFor
\For{each bond $pq$ between two vertices at depth $\ell$ and each pair of disjoint shortest paths from $s$ to $p$ and to $q$}
\State Close the two paths and the bond into a candidate ring of $2\ell+1$ members.
\EndFor
\State Keep each candidate of at most $D$ members that satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:primitive}, testing distances up to $\max(\ell-1,1)$.
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
% snapper:on
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Prism blocks in nanotubes}
\label{sec:prisms}

Ice in a nanotube of small diameter forms stacked prisms whose bases are $j$-membered rings~\cite{koga2001nanotube,goswami2020dseams}.
Two disjoint primitive $j$-rings form a prism block when each member of one ring is bonded to a member of the other in ring order.
For $j=4$ the two rings must also be stacked along the tube, since the side faces of a square tube are squares as well.
The share of the tube filled with $j$-gonal blocks, in percent of its length, is the height
\begin{equation}
  h_j=\frac{100\,N_j\,\bar h}{L},
  \label{eq:height}
\end{equation}
where $N_j$ is the number of blocks, $\bar h=\qty{\dsv{set@height}}{\angstrom}$ is the height of one block and $L$ is the longest cell length, which runs along the tube.
A tube filled with one block type over its whole length has $h_j=100$.

\subsection{Hexagonal and double-diamond cages}
\label{sec:cages}

Two disjoint six-rings $l=(l_0,\dots,l_5)$ and $m=(m_0,\dots,m_5)$ are the basal rings of a hexagonal cage (HC) when three bonds join them at alternate positions,
\begin{equation}
  \{l_p,m_q\},\ \{l_{p+2},m_{q\pm 2}\},\ \{l_{p+4},m_{q\pm 4}\}\in E,
  \label{eq:basal}
\end{equation}
for some $p$ and $q$ and one choice of sign, with indices taken modulo six, and when the three six-cycles that these bonds close with the arcs of $l$ and $m$ are primitive rings~\cite{goswami2020dseams}.
The cage has twelve molecules on five six-rings, two basal and three prismatic (Figure~\ref{fig:cages}a).
A double-diamond cage (DDC) is two adamantane units of ten molecules that share one six-ring, the equator~\cite{haji2015nucleation}.
A six-ring $e=(e_0,\dots,e_5)$ outside every hexagonal cage is an equator when six distinct six-rings, one through each triple $(e_i,e_{i+1},e_{i+2})$, share exactly that triple with $e$.
The three rings through the triples with even $i$ must also close over $e$ into one adamantane unit, and the three with odd $i$ into the other.
The cage then has fourteen molecules on seven six-rings (Figure~\ref{fig:cages}b).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\input{figures/fig-cages}
\caption{\textbf{A hexagonal cage joins two basal six-rings by three bonds, and a double-diamond cage joins two adamantane units through a shared equator.}
(a) A hexagonal cage, whose basal rings $l$ and $m$ are joined by the three bonds of Eq.~\eqref{eq:basal} (orange), and each pair of neighbouring bonds closes a prismatic six-ring, one of which is shaded.
(b) A double-diamond cage, whose equator (orange) is shared by an adamantane unit above it, closed at the molecule $a$, and one below it, closed at $a'$, with one of the six peripheral rings shaded.}
\label{fig:cages}
\end{figure}

d-SEAMS~1 finds the partners of a ring by comparing it with every other six-ring and copies the neighbour list for each comparison.
Its hexagonal-cage search therefore grows with the cube of the number of molecules and its double-diamond search with the square.
We replace the comparisons by an inverted index $I(v)$, the list of six-rings through each molecule $v$.
Every basal partner $m$ of $l$ contains a neighbour of $l_0$ or of $l_1$, so the candidates for $m$ are the rings in $I(w)$ for the neighbours $w$ of these two members, and every peripheral ring of an equator lies in $I(e_i)$ for one of its members.
In a network of bounded degree each list of the index has a bounded length, so both searches visit each six-ring a bounded number of times and are linear in the number $N_6$ of six-rings.
Each six-ring carries the cage types it belongs to, and a molecule is hexagonal when its rings belong only to hexagonal cages, cubic when they belong only to double-diamond cages and mixed when they belong to both.

The double-diamond candidates overlap, because every face of an adamantane unit can be an equator.
Two candidates conflict when they share an adamantane unit, so a set of cages without conflicts is a matching in the graph $H$ whose vertices are the adamantane units and whose edges are the candidates~\cite{schrijver2003combinatorial}.
For ideal ice~Ic, $H$ is bipartite and four-regular, and an Euler split gives a perfect matching~\cite{gabow1976euler}.
For any other bipartite $H$, augmenting paths give a maximum matching~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}, and a vertex cover of the same size certifies it by K\"onig's theorem~\cite{konig1931graphs}.
For a component of other conflicts within the size limit of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}, a branch and bound with a clique-partition bound finds the largest set of cages~\cite{tomita2003maxclique}.
A larger component of this kind marks the packing of its frame as not tight (Section~\sref{sec:s-packing}).
On a frame of \dsv{w@ice-Ic@sites} molecules of ice~Ic, the packing turns \dsv{v@1000@count@two@ddc_rings} equators into \dsv{v@1000@count@two@ddc_cages} cages that cover every molecule.
On the same frame, the search of d-SEAMS~1 reports one cage per equator.
The count in this paper is the packed matching.
On perfect ice~Ic that packed count is $N/2$ cages.
The 2020 search reports one cage for every equatorial candidate, and on perfect ice~Ic that candidate count is $2N$.
Every molecule has four neighbours, and the primitive rings are the $2N$ six-rings of the lattice.
Each six-ring is one equatorial candidate.
Each adamantane unit has four faces, and each face is shared by two units, so the units and the candidates are the vertices and the edges of a four-regular graph $H$.
A four-regular graph on $U$ vertices has $2U$ edges.
The lattice has $2N$ candidates, so $2U=2N$ and $U=N$.
A perfect matching has $U/2$ edges, and each edge is one packed cage, so the packed count is $N/2$.

\subsection{Clathrate cages}
\label{sec:clathrates}

The cages of clathrate hydrates are closed polyhedra whose faces are primitive rings~\cite{jacobson2010amorphous,matsumoto2007topological}.
A set of faces forms a closed cage when every edge lies on two faces, the faces around each vertex form one cycle and the numbers $V$, $E$ and $F$ of its vertices, edges and faces satisfy Euler's relation,
\begin{equation}
  V-E+F=2,
  \label{eq:euler}
\end{equation}
which the cage of twelve pentagons meets with $V=20$, $E=30$ and $F=12$.
A cage is named by its face signature, such as $5^{12}6^{2}$ for the large cage of structure~I and $5^{12}6^{4}$ for that of structure~II, and only faces whose edges lie in the length window of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} enter the search.

\subsection{Carrying rings and cage labels between frames}
\label{sec:update}

Let $G$ and $G'$ be the bond graphs of two consecutive frames, and let $X$ be the set of molecules that are ends of a bond present in one graph and absent from the other.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:local} Let $R$ be a ring of at most seven members with $R\cap X=\emptyset$.
Then $R$ is a primitive ring of $G$ if and only if it is a primitive ring of $G'$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{pf}
Every bond between two members of $R$ has both ends outside $X$, so it is present in both graphs, and $R$ is a cycle of $G$ exactly when it is a cycle of $G'$.
For $|R|\le 7$ the ring distance is $d_R(u,v)\le 3$, so a shortcut between two members has at most two bonds.
Suppose that $R$ is primitive in one graph and has a shortcut $P$ in the other.
Then $P$ is not a path of the first graph, so it contains a changed bond, and both ends of that bond lie in $X$.
Each bond of a path of one or two bonds between members of $R$ has a member of $R$ as an end, so $R$ meets $X$, a contradiction.
\end{pf}

When the search runs to a largest ring size $D\ge 8$, a shortcut can contain a changed bond in its middle while the ring avoids $X$.
The program then replaces $X$ by the molecules within $\lfloor(\lfloor D/2\rfloor-2)/2\rfloor$ bonds of $X$ in the union of the two graphs, which is one bond for $8\le D\le 11$ (Section~\sref{sec:s-locality}).
Algorithm~\ref{alg:update} uses Proposition~\ref{prop:local} for rings of up to seven members.
Every primitive ring of $G$ that avoids $X$ is carried, and every primitive ring of $G'$ that meets $X$ is found by the search from its lowest member in $X$.
That search runs over all of $G'$ and keeps only the rings whose lowest member in $X$ is its start.
The carried rings and the found rings are therefore the primitive rings of $G'$, each once, and the update returns the ring set of a full search on every frame (Section~\sref{sec:s-update}).
The search from $T$ costs as much as Algorithm~\ref{alg:rings} on $|T|$ vertices, but the bond difference and the carried set cost a pass over the neighbour rows of all $n$ molecules.
The update runs that full search when the frames differ in size or when $T$ is the whole vertex set.

\begin{algorithm}[tbp]
\caption{Update of the primitive rings of up to seven members from $G$ to $G'$.}
\label{alg:update}
% snapper:off
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Compare the neighbour rows of $G$ and $G'$ and collect the changed bonds and the set $X$ of their ends.
\If{the frames differ in size}
\State Run Algorithm~\ref{alg:rings} on $G'$ and return its rings.
\EndIf
\State Build $T$, the vertices within reach of a changed row in either frame.
\If{$T$ is the whole vertex set}
\State Run Algorithm~\ref{alg:rings} on $G'$ and return its rings.
\EndIf
\State Remove every ring of $G$ that has a member in $T$, and keep the others.
\For{each $s\in T$}
\State Run the search of Algorithm~\ref{alg:rings} from $s$ over all of $G'$, and keep the rings whose lowest member in $T$ is $s$.
\EndFor
\State Return the kept rings together with the rings found.
\end{algorithmic}
% snapper:on
\end{algorithm}

The cage labels are carried in the same way.
A six-ring is evaluated again when it is new or when one of its molecules has a changed neighbour row.
It is also evaluated again when one of its molecules lies on, or is bonded to a molecule of, a six-ring that appeared or vanished.
Every other ring keeps its cage partners, with their ring indices renumbered (Section~\sref{sec:s-dirty}).
This update still compares every neighbour row and renumbers the partner lists of all rings, so its cost stays linear in $n$ even for a single changed bond.
It falls back to the full pass when the share of the molecules with a changed row, or of the six-rings that pass through such a molecule or have to be evaluated again, passes the limit of Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}.
After a fallback, it runs the full pass on every later frame until the number of molecules changes or a frame has no six-ring.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

d-SEAMS is a C++20 library with a command-line program, built with Meson~\cite{meson} and tested with Catch2~\cite{catch2}.
The program reads LAMMPS dump, XYZ and CON frames~\cite{thompson2022lammps}, builds the bond graphs with cell lists and the minimum image convention~\cite{goswami2026linkcell,goswami2026minimage} and writes per-molecule labels and per-frame counts for each analysis.
Its commands cover the rings and cages of Section~\ref{sec:defs}, the CHILL and CHILL+ labels, Steinhardt parameters, the $F_4$ order parameter, radial distribution functions, coordination numbers, hydrogen bonds and density profiles~\cite{steinhardt1983bond,moore2010chill,nguyen2015identification,rodger1996f4}.
A C interface carries the same analyses to the Python bindings of PydSEAMSlib~\cite{pydseamslib2026}, to R and Julia packages and to the Lua library yodaStruct~\cite{yodastruct2026}.
A LAMMPS compute calls this interface to assign CHILL+ labels during a run.

The carried rings and cage labels of Section~\ref{sec:update} are the default for a trajectory, and an option rebuilds every frame from the start.
Both modes write identical output files on the nucleation trajectory of Section~\ref{sec:nucleation}, and the test suite compares each update with the full search on perturbed frames and on a short trajectory of mW ice.
A randomized test shifts every frame of its test data by half a cell and by random vectors, which must leave the cages unchanged.
It also permutes the molecules, which must leave the number of cages unchanged.
The program installs through pixi or Nix~\cite{pixi,dolstra2004nix}, and Section~\sref{sec:s-repro} lists the commands that produce every figure and table of this paper.

\subsection{Cages in a nucleation trajectory}

Of the \dsv{n@cutoff@gain@ice} molecules that it gains, \qty{\dsv{n@cutoff@gain@cubic@pct}}{\percent} are cubic, \qty{\dsv{n@cutoff@gain@both@pct}}{\percent} are mixed and \qty{\dsv{n@cutoff@gain@hexagonal@pct}}{\percent} are hexagonal.
The growing ice is thus built mostly from double-diamond cages and from molecules that both cage types share.
A mixed molecule lies on rings of both cage types, and the share of mixed molecules in the ice rises over the run on both graphs.
The seeded graph labels fewer molecules than the cutoff graph in the initial frame and about as many in the last, and its shares of the gain differ from those of the cutoff graph by at most \num{\dsv{n@gain@maxdiff@pct}} percentage points.
This stacking-disordered growth agrees with the mixed cubic and hexagonal nuclei found for mW water and for TIP4P/Ice~\cite{moore2011structural,lupi2017stacking,haji2015nucleation}.

\subsection{Strong and weak scaling}
\label{sec:scaling}

The comparisons above pin every process to one thread.
This section raises the Open Multi-Processing (OpenMP) thread count through the powers of two up to the physical cores of the machine in Table~\ref{tab:hardware}, on one release build.
A simultaneous multithreading point is measured only when the hardware-thread count exceeds the physical core count.
Each point is the type 7 median of \num{\dsv{sc@calls}} calls, and the interquartile range (IQR) is the type 7 third quartile minus the first.
No call is dropped.
The full run reads the dump, builds the cutoff neighbour list and runs the analysis.
The kernel time is that analysis without the read and without the neighbour list.
Ring analysis is the primitive-ring search and the bulk cage classification.
Cluster analysis is the cubic hexagonal interfacial liquid classifier (CHILL), in the extended CHILL+ form, and the largest ice-like cluster.
The frames are replicas of the mW crystal and of a bulk TIP4P/2005 ice cell.
That cell contains oxygen, hydrogen, hydrogen and the M site, and each replica fills the periodic box.
The confined water column in the repository is a separate case and is not this ladder.
One replica of each crystal has at least \num{110000} molecules: \num{\dsv{sc@mw@n}} mW molecules and \num{\dsv{sc@tip4p@n}} TIP4P/2005 molecules.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

On one core, d-SEAMS assigns the topological ice structures of a \dsv{hl@n}-molecule ice~Ic frame in \qty{\dsv{hl@two@ms}}{\milli\second}, against \qty{\dsv{hl@one@s}}{\second} for d-SEAMS~1, at a cost that grows linearly with the number of molecules in a network of bounded degree.
On \dsv{sc@physical} physical cores the full ring analysis of \num{\dsv{sc@mw@n}} mW molecules speeds up by \num{\dsv{sc@strong@mw@rings@speedup}}, a parallel efficiency of \qty{\dsv{sc@strong@mw@rings@eff}}{\percent} (Section~\ref{sec:scaling}).
The gain grows with the size of the frame because most of it comes from the index from molecules to rings, which turns the cubic pair loop of the hexagonal-cage search into a linear pass.

For any ring size, the rings carried between frames equal those of a full search, and for rings of up to seven members only the ends of changed bonds are searched again.
With three changed bonds on a frame of a nucleation run, the ring update is \dsv{hl@ringgain} times faster than a full search.
The ring update searches the molecules in $T$ and takes a full pass only when $T$ is every molecule.
The cage-label update stays linear in the number of molecules because it compares every neighbour row.

In all \dsv{hl@matched} completed matched comparisons with OVITO, freud, pyscal, matscipy and the CHILL+ library, d-SEAMS is the faster code, while local diamond-structure classifiers run up to \dsv{hl@local@max} times faster than its cage labels.
Applied to a nucleation run of mW water, the cage labels show the ice growing mostly from cubic and mixed molecules on both bond graphs.

The next step is a cage-label update that reads only the changed bonds, without a pass over every neighbour row.
With such an update, the LAMMPS compute that assigns CHILL+ labels during a run can also assign cage labels on every step.
